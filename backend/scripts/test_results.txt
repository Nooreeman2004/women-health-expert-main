Query: What are the common symptoms of PCOS?
RAG Enabled: True
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Response:
Things to Consider

1. Irregular or absent periods. Some people notice that their menstrual cycle becomes unpredictable, with longer or shorter intervals between periods, or they may stop having periods for several months at a time.

2. Excess hair growth. Hair may grow more quickly or become thicker in areas where it is usually fine, such as the face, chest, or back. This is sometimes called hirsutism.

3. Acne or oily skin. Breakouts can appear on the face, chest, or back, especially during or after puberty. The skin may feel oily or feel like it is not clearing up as quickly as before.

4. Weight changes. Many people with this condition experience difficulty losing weight, or they may notice that their body shape changes even if their diet and exercise habits stay the same.

5. Fertility challenges. It can be harder to become pregnant, and the time to conception may be longer than usual. Some people also experience irregular ovulation.

6. Mood or mental health changes. Feelings of anxiety, depression, or low self‑esteem can be more common. Body image concerns may also arise because of changes in hair, skin, or weight.

7. Other signs. Some people notice a feeling of fullness in the abdomen, bloating, or mild pelvic discomfort that isn’t related to a normal menstrual cycle.

If you see several of these signs, it can be helpful to talk with a healthcare professional. They can discuss ways to manage symptoms, review lifestyle changes, and talk about monitoring your health.

TEST: Pregnancy Query - Morning Sickness
================================================================================
Query: I'm pregnant and experiencing morning sickness. What can I do?
RAG Enabled: True
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Response:
Morning sickness is a common part of early pregnancy, and many people find that small changes can make a big difference in how they feel. Here are a few ideas you might try:

1. Eat small, frequent meals. Instead of three large meals a day, try nibbling on a piece of toast, a handful of crackers, or a small fruit snack every couple of hours. Keeping your stomach from getting too empty can help reduce nausea.

2. Choose bland, low‑fat foods. Plain crackers, rice, bananas, or applesauce are often easier on the stomach than fried or heavily seasoned dishes. If you’re craving something sweet, a small piece of fruit or a few raisins can be soothing.

3. Stay hydrated. Sip water, clear broth, or a lightly flavored electrolyte drink throughout the day. If you find it hard to drink plain water, try adding a slice of lemon or a splash of fruit juice for taste.

4. Watch out for strong smells. Cooking smells, perfumes, or even the scent of certain foods can trigger nausea. If you notice a particular odor makes you feel sick, try to avoid it or keep the room well ventilated.

5. Try ginger in gentle, non‑medication forms. A small cup of ginger tea, ginger‑flavored chewing gum, or a few ginger candies can sometimes calm the stomach. If you’re not sure whether ginger is right for you, a quick chat with your prenatal care provider can help.

6. Rest when you need it. Fatigue can worsen nausea, so give yourself permission to take short naps or simply sit quietly when you feel the urge to vomit.

7. Vitamin C can be helpful, especially if you’re feeling a bit weak or short of breath. A natural source like a small glass of orange juice or a handful of berries is a gentle way to add it to your diet.

8. If you find that you’re vomiting frequently—more than a few times a day—or if you’re losing weight, feeling dizzy, or can’t keep fluids down, it’s a good idea to let your healthcare provider know. They can check that you and the baby are staying healthy.

Remember, many people find that morning sickness improves as the pregnancy progresses, often easing by the end of the second trimester. In the meantime, experimenting with these simple strategies may help you feel a little better. If you try something and it doesn’t help, or if you’re worried about any symptom, reaching out to your prenatal care team is always a safe choice.

TEST: Contraception Information
================================================================================
Query: What are the different types of birth control available?
RAG Enabled: True
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Response:
Things to consider when looking at birth control options

1. Barrier methods keep sperm from reaching the egg. The most common examples are condoms, diaphragms, cervical caps, and sponges. They are easy to use at home and can also reduce the risk of infections that can be spread through sex.

2. Hormonal methods use natural or synthetic hormones to prevent ovulation, thicken cervical mucus, or thin the lining of the uterus. These methods are available in several forms, such as a patch that you change weekly, an implant that stays in place for a few years, or a ring that you leave in for a month. They can also be combined with other benefits, like fewer menstrual cramps or a lighter flow, for some people.

3. Intrauterine devices (IUDs) are small, T‑shaped devices that a provider places inside the uterus. They are long‑acting, lasting from a few years up to ten years depending on the type. IUDs can be hormonal or non‑hormonal, and they work mainly by altering the environment inside the uterus so that sperm cannot fertilize an egg.

4. Sterilization is a permanent option. For people who do not want children in the future, procedures such as tubal ligation (for people with tubes) or vasectomy (for partners) are performed by a medical professional. These are typically considered when a person is sure they do not want more children.

5. Natural methods rely on tracking the body’s signals to predict fertile days. This can include monitoring basal body temperature, cervical mucus, or using calendar calculations. These methods require careful daily monitoring and a good understanding of the cycle to be effective.

6. Emergency contraception can be used after unprotected sex or a known failure of another method. It is most effective when taken as soon as possible and can be obtained over the counter at a pharmacy. It works by delaying ovulation or preventing fertilization.

If you want more detail on any of these categories or how they might fit your lifestyle, feel free to let me know.

TEST: Menopause Query
================================================================================
Query: What are the signs of menopause and how can I manage symptoms?
RAG Enabled: True
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Response:
Signs of Menopause

1. Your periods may become irregular. You might notice longer gaps between cycles, or sometimes a period may be lighter or heavier than usual.
2. Hot flashes and night sweats are common. These are sudden feelings of warmth that can make you sweat and feel flushed, sometimes disrupting sleep.
3. You may notice changes in sleep patterns. It can be harder to fall asleep or stay asleep, and you might feel more tired during the day.
4. Mood shifts can occur. Some people feel more irritable, anxious, or sad, while others notice a change in emotional sensitivity.
5. Vaginal dryness or discomfort during sexual activity may happen as hormone levels change.
6. You might experience changes in weight or body composition, such as increased belly fat or a slower metabolism.
7. Some people notice a decrease in libido or changes in sexual desire.
8. Cognitive changes like occasional forgetfulness or difficulty concentrating can be reported.

Managing Symptoms

1. Keep a symptom tracker for a few weeks before you talk to a health professional. Write down what you experience, how intense it feels, how often it happens, and if anything seems to make it better or worse. This helps you and your provider see patterns.
2. Exercise regularly. Activities like walking, swimming, or light strength training can help keep your bones strong, improve mood, and reduce hot flashes for some people.
3. Eat a balanced diet. Focus on foods rich in calcium, magnesium, and vitamin D to support bone health. Whole grains, fruits, vegetables, and lean protein are good choices.
4. Stay hydrated. Drinking water throughout the day can help with hot flashes and keep skin healthy.
5. Create a cool sleeping environment. Use breathable bedding, keep the room at a comfortable temperature, and consider a fan or a cool pack if you wake up hot.
6. Practice relaxation techniques. Deep breathing, gentle stretching, or mindfulness meditation can help reduce stress and calm hot flashes.
7. Use water‑based lubricants if vaginal dryness causes discomfort. They are safe to use during sexual activity.
8. Consider herbal teas or natural supplements that are often used for menopause support. For example, some people find that a small amount of black‑cohosh or evening primrose oil helps, but talk with a healthcare provider before starting anything new.
9. Avoid caffeine, alcohol, and very hot beverages if they seem to trigger hot flashes. Try to keep a cool drink nearby during the day.
10. Keep a regular sleep routine. Go to bed and wake up at the same time each day, and create a calming pre‑sleep ritual such as reading or gentle stretching.
11. Stay connected with friends, family, or support groups

Query: What vitamins should I take for better reproductive health?

Response:
Things to Consider

1. A balanced diet is the first step toward healthy reproductive function. Foods that are naturally rich in vitamins and minerals—such as leafy greens, nuts, seeds, fish, eggs, and whole grains—provide many of the nutrients you need.

2. If you feel you might not be getting enough of certain nutrients, a supplement can help fill gaps. Commonly recommended vitamins for reproductive health include:

 1. Vitamin D. Adequate vitamin D supports hormone balance and bone health, which is important for menstrual regularity. Many adults aim for about 600–800 IU per day, but individual needs can vary.
 2. B‑complex vitamins, especially B6 and B12. These help the body produce energy and support mood, which can be useful during hormonal changes.
 3. Folic acid (folate). This vitamin is essential for healthy cell division and is often advised for women who are planning pregnancy or who are pregnant.
 4. Iron. Iron supports healthy blood flow and can help prevent anemia, especially if you have heavy periods.
 5. Zinc. Zinc is involved in hormone production and immune function, and it can support normal ovulation.
 6. Omega‑3 fatty acids (from fish oil or plant sources). These healthy fats can help regulate inflammation and support overall reproductive health.

3. When taking supplements, be mindful of potential interactions. For example, very high supplemental doses of vitamin C and vitamin B6 might affect how the body processes estrogen. If you are using hormone replacement therapy or any hormonal medication, it’s worth discussing supplement doses with a health professional.

4. Lifestyle factors also play a big role. Regular physical activity, maintaining a healthy weight, managing stress, and getting enough sleep can all support reproductive health. Avoid smoking and limit alcohol intake, as these can negatively impact fertility and hormone balance.

5. If you’re planning a pregnancy, talk with a provider about a prenatal vitamin that includes folic acid, iron, calcium, and other key nutrients. If you have specific concerns—such as irregular periods, heavy bleeding, or infertility—consider seeing a healthcare professional for personalized guidance.

Feel free to let me know if you’d like more detail on any of these vitamins or on how to combine them with a healthy lifestyle.

Query: What is endometriosis?
RAG Enabled: False
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Response:
Endometriosis is a condition that involves tissue similar to the lining inside the uterus growing outside of it. That tissue can be found on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, the lining of the pelvic cavity, or other nearby organs. When the body’s cycle changes the blood that normally leaves the uterus can flow back through the fallopian tubes and reach this extra‑uterine tissue. The tissue reacts to the hormone changes in the same way the lining inside the uterus does, which can cause inflammation, pain, and sometimes scar tissue to form.

People with endometriosis often notice a few patterns:

1. Pain that may feel like cramps, a dull ache, or sharp pressure, especially around the time of a period. The pain can start before a period, last for a few days, and sometimes persist afterward. 
2. Pain that worsens during sexual activity, bowel movements, or bladder use. 
3. Fatigue or feeling unwell during the menstrual cycle. 
4. In some cases, difficulties with pregnancy or unexplained infertility, though many people with endometriosis can conceive. 
5. A feeling of fullness or pressure in the lower abdomen or pelvis. 

It’s important to remember that everyone experiences it differently. Some people have mild symptoms that rarely interfere with daily life, while others have more severe discomfort that can affect work, school, or relationships. Because the symptoms can overlap with many other conditions, it can be challenging to know exactly what’s causing them. If you suspect you might have endometriosis or are experiencing persistent pain or other concerning signs, it can be helpful to talk with a healthcare professional who can explore the possibility and discuss options for managing the symptoms.
